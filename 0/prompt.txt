## Literature check: LLM time-series inputs, usefulness, OpenTSLM, ensembling

### 1. LLMs reading numbers as text tokens

**Claim.** LLMTime encodes a series as digit strings and treats forecasting as next-token prediction; GPT-3 and LLaMA-2 then extrapolate zero-shot at a level "comparable to or exceeding" purpose-built models. The paper stresses that "careful tokenization is important for good forecasting with LLMs": GPT-3's BPE chunks "4223563042235630" as [422,35,630], so they insert spaces between digits, whereas "in many new open-source LLMs (e.g. LLaMA), numbers are tokenized into individual digits by default" and spacing hurts LLaMA-2.
**Citation.** Gruver, Finzi, Qiu, Wilson. "Large Language Models Are Zero-Shot Time Series Forecasters." NeurIPS 2023. arXiv:2310.07820.
**Follow-ups.** Singh and Strouse, "Tokenization counts: the impact of tokenization on arithmetic in frontier LLMs," arXiv:2402.14903 (2024): GPT-3.5/4 "have separate tokens for each 1-, 2-, and 3-digit numbers" and right-to-left chunking "leads to largely improved performance". Llama 3 uses the same 0 to 999 chunking (Llama 2 was digit-level), documented in beren.io, "Integer tokenization is now much less insane" (2024). Zhang et al., "From Tokenizer Bias to Backbone Capability: A Controlled Study of LLMs for Time Series Forecasting," arXiv:2504.08818 (2025, rev. 2026): learned tokenizer/detokenizer pairs "often overfit to the specific data distribution, thereby masking the intrinsic predictive capability of the LLM backbone."
**Relation.** Our plain-number prompt is the LLMTime recipe applied to Llama 3.2; delay minutes are small integers (mostly under 1000), so each value is one token, which is the regime where text encoding is cheapest and cleanest.

### 2. Do LLM components help at all?

**Claim.** Tan et al. ablate three LLM forecasters and find that "removing the LLM component or replacing it with a basic attention layer does not degrade forecasting performance"; pretrained LLMs "do no better than models trained from scratch" and "do not assist in few-shot settings." Time-LLM (Jin et al., ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.01728) is the opposing claim: reprogrammed frozen LLMs "outperform state-of-the-art, specialized forecasting models" and "excel in both few-shot and zero-shot learning."
**Citation.** Tan, Merrill, Gupta, Althoff, Hartvigsen. "Are Language Models Actually Useful for Time Series Forecasting?" NeurIPS 2024 (Spotlight). arXiv:2406.16964.
**Rebuttal.** Qiu et al., "Rethinking the Role of LLMs in Time Series Forecasting," arXiv:2602.14744 (Feb 2026): on roughly 8 billion observations, "LLM4TS indeed improves forecasting performance," with "pretraining critical under distribution shifts"; prior negative results "stem from limited evaluation settings."
**Relation.** Our "pretrained encoder vs random init makes no difference" mirrors Tan et al.'s from-scratch finding; the added learned modality adapter is exactly the component they show to be dispensable.

### 3. OpenTSLM

**Claim.** Two variants: SoftPrompt (PatchTST-style patch encoder projected into the token stream, LLM tuned with LoRA) and Flamingo (gated cross-attention, frozen backbone). The v1 abstract states Flamingo "matches OpenTSLM-SoftPrompt in performance and outperforms on longer sequences, while maintaining stable memory" (110 GB vs 40 GB on ECG-QA with Llama-3B; v3 reports &gt;180 GB vs up to 60 GB). Text-only baselines write the series as tokens: fine-tuned Llama-3.2-3B gets 60.44 F1 on HAR-CoT vs 65.44 for OpenTSLM, but collapses on Sleep-CoT (5.86 F1) and goes OOM on ECG-QA ("80k tokens"). There is no ablation on encoder initialisation or on removing curriculum stages.
**Citation.** Langer, Kaar, Rosenblattl, Xu, et al. (Stanford, ETH Zurich, UIUC, Google Research). "OpenTSLM: Time-Series Language Models for Reasoning over Multivariate Medical Text- and Time-Series Data." arXiv:2510.02410 (Oct 2025, v3 Feb 2026; OpenReview PlYAAdwBy1).
**Relation.** OpenTSLM's advantage over text-only inputs appears only where series are long; our four short series sit in the regime (HAR) where the gap is a few F1 points, which is consistent with plain text winning at equal steps.

### 4. Combining with gradient boosting, horizon-dependent blending

**Claim.** No paper was found blending LLM forecasts with XGBoost by lead time. The closest precedent is the forecast-combination literature: Wang, Hyndman, Li, Kang, "Forecast combinations: an over 50-year review," International Journal of Forecasting 2023, arXiv:2205.04216 (time-varying weights); Cerqueira and Torgo, "Multi-output Ensembles for Multi-step Forecasting," arXiv:2306.14563 (per-horizon vs shared weights on 3,568 series; "as the horizon increases, most approaches struggle to outperform" equal weights); Ray et al., arXiv:2201.12387 (COVID Hub: per-horizon weights help at 1 to 2 weeks, hurt at 3 to 4). Domain baseline: Elliker et al., "RIDE: An Open Dataset and Benchmark for Train Delay Prediction," arXiv:2606.05070 (Belgian network, graph neural nets best, results broken down by horizon).
**Relation.** Our lead-time-dependent blend is a standard horizon-varying combination; the literature warns that per-horizon weights overfit when the calibration set is small, so a smooth weight-vs-lead-time schedule is the defensible choice.

Sources: [arXiv:2310.07820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820), [arXiv:2402.14903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14903), [beren.io](https://www.beren.io/2024-05-11-Integer-tokenization-is-now-much-less-insane/), [arXiv:2504.08818](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08818), [arXiv:2406.16964](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964), [arXiv:2310.01728](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728), [arXiv:2602.14744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14744), [arXiv:2510.02410](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02410), [OpenTSLM GitHub](https://github.com/StanfordBDHG/OpenTSLM), [arXiv:2205.04216](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04216), [arXiv:2306.14563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14563), [arXiv:2201.12387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12387), [arXiv:2606.05070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05070)</result>

## Literature and landscape check: DB per-stop delay prediction

### 1. Academic work

1. **Nair, Hoang, Laumanns, Chen, Cogill, Szabó, Walter (2019), Transportation Research Part C 104.** Ensemble (context-aware random forest, kernel regression, simulation) on Deutsche Bahn's full network (about 25,000 trains/day). Baseline is the published timetable. Reports 25% better "correctness" (share within 1 min) and 50% lower RMSE; RMSE grows from about 110 s (0 to 10 min ahead) to 400 to 500 s (180+ min ahead). [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019TRPC..104..196N/abstract)

2. **Spanninger, Trivella, Büchel, Corman (2022), J. Rail Transport Planning &amp; Management 22.** Review of 71 papers. Reported real-time MAEs: Kecman &amp; Goverde 2015 (NS) about 40 s beyond 20 min; Oneto et al. 2018 (Italian RFI, extreme learning machines) 1.5 to 2.5 min for 1 to 5 stations ahead; Corman &amp; Kecman 2018 (Bayesian networks, Sweden) 30 to 100 s over 0 to 60 min. Most data-driven work is deterministic; probabilistic outputs are rare. [PDF](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/278073033/1_s2.0_S2210970622000166_main.pdf)

3. **Elliker, Le Bail, Mantoux, Read, Vanier (2026), RIDE benchmark, arXiv 2606.05070.** Belgian network, 94.5M events, temporal split (train 2023 to 2024, test 2025), predict next 15 events (about 40 min). Test MAE: persistence 1.61 min, XGBoost 1.28, LSTM 1.24, GNN 1.23; at 45+ min all models degrade to 4 to 5 min MAE. The paper calls persistence "surprisingly strong" and "difficult to beat by a large margin". [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05070)

4. **Sobrie, Verschelde, Hennebel, Roets (2023), EJOR 310.** LSTM encoder-decoder for real-time delay prediction at Infrabel; beats the rule-based operational forecaster by 18% (23% in complex situations). [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221723002631)

5. **Spanninger, Wiedemann, Corman (2024), Transportation Research Part C.** Uncertainty-aware neural nets (test-time dropout, loss attenuation) on Swiss data; predictability decays exponentially with horizon and depends on train category (regional vs intercity). Closest published analogue to a distributional target. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0968090X24000846)

6. **Kampere &amp; Alsahag (2025), arXiv 2507.11776.** XGBoost with network centrality features on NS data; reports limited performance under non-simultaneous (forward-in-time) testing. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11776)

### 2. Open-source DB projects

7. **Bahn-Vorhersage (Döllmann et al.), GitLab and Augsburg OPUS dataset paper (2025).** Polls the DB Timetable API for all stations since September 2021 (about 700 GB, ODbL). Predicts transfer-success probabilities from train type, time, route so far and forecast time; no published MAE, only the claim that its punctuality statistics match official DB figures. [OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/year/2026/docId/127213), [GitLab](https://gitlab.com/bahnvorhersage/bahnvorhersage)

8. **piebro/deutsche-bahn-data (Hugging Face, CC BY 4.0).** IRIS timetable scrapes, roughly 226M rows, top 100 stations from 2024-07, all stations since 2025-11, with `delay_in_min` and cancellation flags. No accompanying model or accuracy. [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/piebro/deutsche-bahn-data)

### 3. DB's own forecasting

9. **DB "RI-Prognose" (since 2018) and heise (2025).** ML trained on historical patterns plus live network messages, GPS positions, dispatcher inputs; about 150M forecasts/day refreshed every two minutes. DB reports 74% accuracy for stop cancellations and platform changes (target 80% by 2027) and aims to announce stop cancellations 60 min ahead. No delay MAE is published. [DB](https://www.deutschebahn.com/de/kuenstlicheintelligenz-6898594), [heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Forecasting-machine-How-AI-is-set-to-improve-rail-travel-information-10343595.html)

### 4. Reference statistics

10. Official punctuality (under 6 min): long-distance 60.1% in 2025 (62.5% in 2024), regional 88.7%, all passenger trains 88.0%; H1 2026 long-distance 58.7% with 8% of long-distance trains fully or partly cancelled (ministry answer to a parliamentary inquiry, August 2026). Cancellations are excluded from the punctuality figure. [eisenbahn.de](https://eisenbahn.de/geramond-vgb/jahresbilanz-2025-puenktlichkeit-der-deutschen-bahn-sinkt-weiter_17653), [schiene.de](https://www.schiene.de/news-12105/Etwa-jeder-zwoelfte-DB-Fernzug-ganz-oder-teilweise-ausgefallen.html)

### Where our numbers sit

Our 25% of stops at least 6 min late is a plausible blend of about 40% for long-distance and about 11% for regional stops; 5% cancelled is consistent with 8% partial-or-full long-distance cancellations diluted by regional and S-Bahn traffic. On error, the field is not directly comparable: published MAEs of 0.5 to 2.5 min come from short horizons (next station, under 40 min) on denser, more punctual networks, and RIDE shows 4 to 5 min MAE once horizons exceed 45 min. Our median AE of 3.4 min and CRPS of 2.7 min over horizons up to hours on a German summer with 60% long-distance punctuality is therefore in the expected band, but should be reported per horizon bucket to be credible. The baseline the field expects is persistence (last known delay carried forward), with XGBoost as the standard learned reference; RIDE makes explicit that beating persistence by a wide margin is hard, so stating our gain over it per horizon is the key comparison. Probabilistic per-stop output with CRPS is rare in this literature (Spanninger 2024, Corman &amp; Kecman 2018 are the nearest), which is a legitimate novelty claim for the write-up.</result>
