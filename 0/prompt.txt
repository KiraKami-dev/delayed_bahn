Another model gave me this: "I've read the design notes, the evaluation, the run log and the model code. Here's the explanation, written so you can present it.

How we trained the delay model

1. The problem in one sentence

Given everything known right now about a train, predict how many minutes late it will arrive at your stop. Don't give one number; give a full "weather forecast" of likely delays. From two of those forecasts you get the chance of making your connection.

2. The big picture

Public DB data (10 months, 142M stop records)
        │  build.py: cut into ~300k "moments in a train's life"
        ▼
One example = one train, one target stop, one moment in time
        │  prompts.py: written up as a short text + a few number series
        ▼
Small language model (Llama 3.2, 3B) + OpenTSLM time-series add-on
        │  train.py: fine-tuned on H100 GPUs (Nebius), 12,000 steps
        ▼
Answer: "Answer: 7": we read the probability of every minute 0..999
        │  connection.py: combine arriving and departing train
        ▼
"about +8 min, likely 3 to 20, 72% chance of making your change"

3. Step by step, in plain terms

The data. We used an open dataset of Deutsche Bahn timetables and live changes (Hugging Face piebro/deutsche-bahn-data), Nov 2025 to Aug 2026, covering every station. From 142 million stop records we sampled about 300,000 training examples. Each one freezes a train at one moment: "the ICE just left Kassel 4 minutes late; how late will it reach Göttingen?"

What the model gets to see. We only use information from before that moment; nothing from the future leaks in.
- This train's delay at each stop so far.
- How this route usually does at the target stop (last 30 days).
- How late the target station and the whole network are running (last 72 hours).
- Later additions that helped: the previous trains on the same route today; how much this train number gained or lost on the remaining stretch over the last 28 days; trains just ahead on the same corridor.
- A plain-English "rationale" summarising those facts, such as "Over the last stops the delay has grown from 2 to 4 minutes..."
- XGBoost's forecast written as a sentence. XGBoost is a strong classic, non-AI statistics model, and we put its prediction into the prompt ("A statistical model expects about 5 minutes late, likely 1 to 12"). This is called stacking: the language model's job becomes "correct the statistician using what you read".

What the model answers. It always ends with Answer: <minutes> or Answer: cancelled. There's a neat trick here: in Llama's vocabulary, every number from 0 to 999 is a single "word". So at the answer position the model's probabilities over those 1,000 words are directly a probability distribution over minutes. One pass through the model gives the typical delay, the 10 to 90% range, the chance of cancellation, and the chance of being more than 6 minutes late.

The model. We used Llama 3.2 3B (small enough to run on a laptop) with OpenTSLM, a published add-on from Stanford/ETH that lets language models read time series.

"Embeddings", explained for non-experts. A language model never sees letters. Every word becomes a list of numbers (a vector) that encodes its meaning. OpenTSLM adds a small extra network (the encoder) that does the same for a time series:
1. It chops the series into chunks of 4 values.
2. It turns each chunk into a vector.
3. It translates that vector into the language model's "word-vector space".

These "soft tokens" then sit in the prompt as if they were words. Result: our series cost about 45 soft tokens instead of about 480 tokens written out as numbers.

We fed series to the model in several ways and compared them:
- text: numbers written into the prompt, no encoder.
- series: through the encoder only.
- split: recent facts as text, long history through the encoder.
- none: no series at all, as a control.

Fine-tuning. We didn't retrain all 3 billion weights. LoRA adds small trainable adapters (24M parameters, under 1% of the model) and trains only those plus the encoder. It's cheap, fast, and the base model's language ability stays intact. Without fine-tuning the model couldn't do the task: CRPS was much worse, and none of its answers followed the format.

How it learns (the loss). A plain language model treats "guessed 9, truth 8" exactly as wrong as "guessed 500". We added a CRPS term, which penalises a guess by how far it is from the truth. That's also the score we evaluate with. We first tried "soft targets" (blurring the correct answer); they were measurably worse, so we dropped them.

Compute. Each run was one H100 GPU job on Nebius. A 2,000-step experiment took about 20 minutes, so we could run many in parallel overnight. The finals ran for 12,000 steps (about 96k examples, roughly half the training data once).

4. How we checked it honestly

- Time split, no shuffling. Train on Nov to Apr, tune on May, test on Jun to Aug. We also scraped September after training was frozen: an out-of-time test that nothing was fitted on.
- Baselines.
  - "Delay stays the same" (persistence).
  - Route history.
  - XGBoost trained on exactly the same features. That last one is the model bahnvorhersage.de uses, so it's the real bar to beat. Whenever we gave our model new data, we retrained XGBoost on it too.
- Metrics.
  - CRPS: quality of the whole forecast, lower is better.
  - Average miss in minutes.
  - Share within 1 minute.
  - Whether the 10 to 90% range really contains the truth about 80% of the time.
- Significance. For every comparison we used a paired bootstrap with 95% intervals. We only call a difference "real" if the interval excludes zero.

6. Key design decisions and why

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                          Decision                           │                                                        Why                                                        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Predict per train and stop, not per station                 │ The train's own delay so far explains far more than any static grouping (measured)                                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Exact minutes plus a distribution, not "late / on time"     │ Travellers want a minute value; the distribution gives any range for free; buckets add arbitrary cut-offs         │
│ buckets                                                     │                                                                                                                   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Transfer times applied after the model                      │ Transfer times are static, and the data doesn't record who made which connection; the model stays reusable        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Time-based split plus a September test                      │ A random split leaks the future; September is a test nobody can call leaked                                       │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Stack XGBoost's forecast into the prompt                    │ It was the biggest single boost; the language model learns to correct a strong forecast instead of starting from  │
│                                                             │ scratch                                                                                                           │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Out-of-fold XGBoost forecasts in training                   │ Otherwise the model would see a forecast fitted on its own row (classic stacking hygiene)                         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ LoRA with a 3B model                                        │ Fits one GPU, trains in minutes, runs on a laptop for the demo                                                    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Delay causes and weather left out                           │ We tested causes: no measurable gain, because they mostly restate the delay the train already shows               │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

7. Be careful here in the presentation

1. The embeddings are not why it wins. This is the most important one. Our controls show the encoder adds about nothing: split, encoder-only and no-series all score the same. The win comes from the language model reading the facts in the rationale plus the stacked XGBoost forecast.
   - The app currently ships the split (embedding) model; the submission is the text model.
   - Honest framing: "The encoder is the compact route: the same accuracy within about 0.01 CRPS, with a tenth of the prompt."
   - This matches published work: Tan et al. (NeurIPS 2024) found time-series add-ons are often dispensable on short series.
2. The gains are real but modest. CRPS improves by about 3%; the average miss drops by about 5 seconds. The best line for the audience is "right to the minute 67% of the time vs 59%".
3. Nobody predicts the rare huge delays. All models share that heavy tail. Our ranges are slightly too wide (about 89% coverage instead of 80%): cautious rather than overconfident.
4. Treating two trains as independent is optimistic when one disruption hits both.
5. db_route/RESULTS.md is a separate teammate experiment with the same recipe on a different task. At 2k steps it only ties the baseline. Don't mix its numbers with ours.
